Profile

I am a Product Engineering Leader with twelve years building native mobile and platform software, spanning product discovery, workflow design, launch scope, release quality, and the architecture underneath all of it.

This portfolio is organized around the work hiring teams need to evaluate quickly: how an ambiguous idea becomes a scoped product bet, how that bet becomes a shippable system, and how technical depth keeps the tradeoffs along the way honest rather than theoretical. On cross-functional work it is also organized around where the risk actually lives: the ownership boundaries between teams, and who gets to decide.

How I Work

Problem Framing

I start from the customer problem and the constraint, not the feature request. My background in behavioral sciences and education is the real leverage here: it shapes how I read user behavior, separate what people say from what they do, and turn a vague goal into a problem statement a team can build against. I write the tradeoffs down — customer value, implementation cost, operational trust — along with what we are choosing not to do, so a decision stays reviewable instead of being remembered differently by everyone a month later.

Launch and Ownership

I scope launches as reviewable increments with an explicit quality bar, then drive them across the functions that have to agree — while keeping clear who actually decides. On cross-stack work the product risk usually sits in the boundaries between teams rather than inside any one of them, so I name those boundaries early and own the integration path across them. Where behavior could change the call, I have defined the KPIs and used them to redirect the work. I treat that instrumentation, the observability around it, and a rollback path as product requirements.

Technical Judgment

I carry enough depth to keep engineering tradeoffs honest. I have architected native mobile clients, shared cross-platform cores, SDKs consumed by external developers, and the API and authentication boundaries between them — so I can tell a stakeholder what a constraint actually costs, question an estimate without guessing, and write a spec that survives contact with the codebase. I use AI-assisted tooling pragmatically, around solid systems and people first.

Community Booking Platform

Community Booking Platform is collaborative OpenForm Studio work with Keys 4 Community (K4C), a nonprofit serving the Triangle area, focused on turning message-and-spreadsheet scheduling into a trustworthy booking workflow. It highlights product and architecture judgment around clear participant roles, server-enforced scheduling rules, administrative oversight, notification workflows, and a shared API contract that supports web delivery now and native clients later.

Public repository: Community Booking Platform

Product judgment:

  • Framed the problem before the solution: the nonprofit was coordinating bookings through group messages and a spreadsheet, so the real failure mode was not a missing calendar but unclear ownership of who had confirmed what.
  • Defined participant roles, administrative oversight, and notification workflows as the product surface, then pushed the scheduling rules into the API so a client could not bypass them.
  • Chose web-first delivery against a native-ready API contract, so the scheduling workflow can be built and reviewed before a native client exists, with native clients still available later.
  • Worked from booking failures observed with the operator rather than designing against a generic calendar abstraction.

Product and technical focus:

  • Web-first delivery with a native-ready architecture, server-owned workflow state, role-based access, scheduling constraints enforced by the API, transactional notifications, OpenAPI contract discipline, Next.js/React/TypeScript, FastAPI/Python, PostgreSQL, Redis-backed jobs, OIDC-oriented auth, and Kotlin Multiplatform planning for later native clients.

Context-Aware AI Integration

Context-Aware AI Integration is in-progress OpenForm Studio work. The product requirement is that generated output stays grounded in the user’s own source material. That trades away raw model range: the product declines answers it cannot ground, on the judgment that an untrustworthy answer costs more than a missing one. The direction is useful AI inside a real mobile workflow.

The intended shape is an iOS-first path with UIKit and SwiftUI, with Android later on a Kotlin Multiplatform shared core and dedicated Compose UI. Context assembly, session state, model adapter boundaries, and grounding checks belong on that shared core, so a client does not own prompt construction or acceptance of ungrounded output.

Public repository: Context-Aware AI Integration

Product judgment:

  • Started from a product requirement rather than a model capability: generated output has to stay grounded in the user’s own source material, or the feature is not trustworthy enough to ship. That trades away raw model range — the product declines answers it cannot ground — on the judgment that an untrustworthy answer costs more than a missing one.
  • The boundary that requirement calls for: context assembly, session state, and grounding checks on the shared core, so no client can accept ungrounded output on its own.
  • Failure behavior is part of the product direction: inference timeout, retry, and fallback paths that keep the workflow usable when the model is slow or wrong.
  • Kept product runtime AI separate from AI delivery tooling, in both the architecture and the story told about it.

Product and technical focus:

  • iOS first with UIKit and SwiftUI, Android follow-on with Kotlin and Jetpack Compose, Kotlin Multiplatform shared core, structured local context, model adapter boundaries, grounding checks, and inference timeout/retry/fallback behavior.

Epic Games Store iOS, Litigation Period

The open question was how the Epic Games Store should show up on iOS from a largely Android-first footprint, during an active App Store dispute. The call was to wrap the existing storefront in native application infrastructure and target a release candidate in roughly five months. A full native rebuild remained the better long-term product, and the wrong call for that window.

After Houseparty, the work was a native iOS Epic Games Store client during active litigation between Epic and Apple over iOS App Store distribution and in-app payment rules. See Epic Games v. Apple and the CourtListener docket.

The iOS client wrapped the Epic Games Store in native application infrastructure, with authorization and user context passed through web bridges and native delegates. That architecture let us move quickly while still respecting mobile expectations around authentication, session handling, app lifecycle, privacy declarations, and App Store submission readiness.

Within roughly five months, that work reached a release candidate ready for Apple review, pending legal and executive approval. It could not move through the normal developer-account path because of the ongoing dispute. The release candidate was delivered, and it left a practical foundation for later EGS Marketplace work.

Product judgment:

  • Took an open strategic question — how the Epic Games Store should show up on iOS from a largely Android-first footprint, during an active App Store dispute — and turned it into a scoped delivery plan.
  • Chose to wrap the existing storefront in native application infrastructure rather than rebuild it, trading long-term native polish for a release candidate inside roughly five months. Rebuilding natively was the better long-term product and the wrong call for the window we actually had.
  • Ran leadership demos as explicit go/no-go decision points so stakeholders could judge progress while legal and platform constraints shifted outside the team’s control.
  • Delivered the intended release candidate, ready for Apple review pending legal and executive approval, and left a practical foundation for later EGS Marketplace work.

The core product idea joined location, shared virtual objects, user inventory, and real-world redemption into a mobile AR experience.

My role was to move the idea from concept to a working investor-facing mobile experience. I researched ARKit after WWDC 2017, presented AR/MR/VR trends for prospective clients and the local technology community, and then worked directly with Robert to build a mobile experience suitable for investor conversations.

Product judgment:
